Plstka wins 1st place at the Techne Competition

Our AI route-optimisation engine cut average collection distance by 28% across the Delta. Here's what we presented, what the judges pushed back on, and where the technology goes next.

Last week we stood in front of a panel of judges in Cairo and explained why a recycling company had spent eight months building a routing engine. We left with first place — but the most useful part of the day was the twenty minutes of questions afterwards.

The problem we pitched

When Plstka started, pickups ran on a fixed calendar. A van left the depot each morning with a list of addresses in roughly the order they were booked, and a driver who knew the city well enough to improvise. It worked, and it did not scale.

By late 2025 we were running four cities and several hundred pickups a week. Two problems had become expensive:

  • Half-empty trips. A van would cross Tanta for a single 4 kg collection because it was booked for that morning.
  • Unpredictable arrival windows. When a route ran long, every household after it waited — and a missed pickup is the fastest way to lose a user.

Both come down to the same thing: we were sequencing stops without any model of what the stops were worth.

What we actually built

The engine is less exotic than "AI" suggests. It is a capacitated vehicle-routing solver with time windows, wrapped around two prediction models that feed it better inputs.

Predicting volume before we arrive

The first model estimates how much material a booking will actually yield, from the categories selected, the household's history, and the time since their last pickup. Users are generous but imprecise — "a few bottles" covers a wide range. Predicting the weight lets the solver decide whether a stop is worth a detour.

Predicting how long a stop takes

The second model estimates service time. A ground-floor pickup in a wide street is not the same job as a fifth-floor walk-up on a market day. Feeding realistic service times into the solver is what turned our arrival windows from optimistic to accurate.

💡 Why not just use a maps API?

We do — for travel times between points. The hard part is not the distance matrix; it is deciding which stops belong on which van on a given day, given capacity, promised time windows and the value of the material. That is the part we had to build.

The results

We ran the engine in shadow mode for six weeks before it made a single real decision — generating routes daily, comparing them to what dispatchers actually did, and only switching over once it was consistently better.

-28%Average collection distance
+19%Pickups per van per day
94%On-time arrival rate

The distance figure is the one that won the competition, but the arrival-window number is the one we care about internally. It is the difference between a service people trust and one they stop booking.

The routing model didn't just cut cost. It made the promise we make to users an honest one. — Presented at the Techne Competition final, March 2026

What the judges pushed back on

Three questions were sharp enough that we are still working through them.

"Your baseline was a human doing it badly." Fair. Our dispatchers were improvising without tools, so beating them by 28% says as much about the old process as the new model. We are now benchmarking against a properly tuned classical solver, and we will publish that comparison even if the gap narrows.

"What happens when the model is wrong?" If volume is badly under-predicted, a van fills up early and the tail of the route gets bumped. We cap this with a conservative capacity buffer, but a judge rightly pointed out that the cost of that failure lands on the household, not on us.

"Does this work in a city you don't know?" Honestly: unproven. Every model here is trained on Delta data. Launching in a new city means months of thinner predictions, and we should say that plainly rather than implying the system arrives fully formed.

Where it goes next

Three things are on the roadmap for the next two quarters:

  1. Live re-routing. Today routes are computed the night before. We want to re-optimise mid-shift when a pickup is cancelled or a road closes.
  2. Business and household routes on one map. Our business platform currently plans commercial collections separately. Merging the two should cut empty running further.
  3. Cold-start for new cities. Transfer learning so a new city inherits sensible priors instead of starting blind.

If you work on vehicle routing, demand prediction or anything adjacent, we would genuinely like to compare notes — and we're hiring for this team.
